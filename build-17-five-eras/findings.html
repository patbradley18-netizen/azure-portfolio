Who was there to serve: six eras of veterans against the whole population of the same age at the same time

March Current Population Survey, 1968–2025 (672,016 veteran records across the six eras). Each era's veterans are pooled over the years in which they were 18–64 and compared with everyone surveyed in the same years — veterans included — reweighted to the cohort's own age mix, so WWII veterans of 1970 are measured against all 45–55-year-olds of 1970, not against 2025 and not against the minority of men who did not serve. Representation index: share among the era's veterans ÷ share among the population the same age; 1.0 means the same share as everyone their age. Bars are 95% intervals. Anchor phrase: who served, against who was there. This page reports representation; it does not argue who should have served.

Race and ethnicity

White non-Hispanic share of each era's veterans against everyone the same age in the same years: WWII 73.5% vs 69.8% (index 1.05, CI +3.4 to +4.0 pts); Korea 79.0% vs 73.9% (index 1.07, CI +4.8 to +5.4 pts); Vietnam 83.4% vs 76.8% (index 1.09, CI +6.4 to +6.8 pts); '75–'90 72.5% vs 67.8% (index 1.07, CI +4.2 to +5.3 pts); Gulf War I 69.2% vs 62.7% (index 1.10, CI +5.8 to +7.2 pts); Post-9/11 66.1% vs 57.9% (index 1.14, CI +7.5 to +9.0 pts). Black: WWII 0.78; Korea 0.79; Vietnam 0.87; '75–'90 1.45; Gulf War I 1.41; Post-9/11 1.12. Hispanic: WWII 0.48; Korea 0.50; Vietnam 0.52; '75–'90 0.50; Gulf War I 0.53; Post-9/11 0.66.

Sex

Veteran status was asked of men only before 1988, so women are measured from 1988 on — for WWII and Korea that means survivors in their 60s and beyond. Women as a share of each era's veterans: WWII 1.4%; Korea 1.8%; Vietnam 2.8%; '75–'90 12.8%; Gulf War I 16.4%; Post-9/11 18.0% — against roughly half of the population in every window (women measured from 1988 on).

Citizenship and generation

Citizenship (1994 on, so the older eras are their later survivors): non-citizens are '75–'90 0.5% vs 7.9% of the population; Gulf War I 0.8% vs 10.7% of the population; Post-9/11 0.8% vs 11.1% of the population. Naturalized citizens: '75–'90 2.3% vs 9.4%; Gulf War I 3.3% vs 9.0%; Post-9/11 4.1% vs 7.0%. Foreign-born: '75–'90 index 0.27; Gulf War I index 0.29; Post-9/11 index 0.39; US-born with a foreign-born parent: '75–'90 index 1.02; Gulf War I index 0.95; Post-9/11 index 0.87.

Education

Education: no-diploma share vs the population the same age — WWII 36.7% vs 40.3% (h -0.07); Korea 22.7% vs 30.3% (h -0.17); Vietnam 7.2% vs 17.0% (h -0.31); '75–'90 3.0% vs 10.4% (h -0.31); Gulf War I 1.2% vs 10.8% (h -0.45); Post-9/11 1.4% vs 10.9% (h -0.43). Bachelor's or more — WWII 7.3% vs 7.2%; Korea 11.0% vs 11.1%; Vietnam 17.5% vs 18.4%; '75–'90 25.5% vs 32.8%; Gulf War I 33.0% vs 34.1%; Post-9/11 30.9% vs 32.3%.

From 2006 the survey records up to four periods; the share of each era's veterans who also served in another era: WWII 0.0%; Korea 0.0%; Vietnam 1.8%; '75–'90 18.9%; Gulf War I 28.4%; Post-9/11 21.0% (pooled over 2006+ only where the window reaches it).

What this cannot say

Representation is not selection: a cohort can differ from the population its age because of who volunteered, who was drafted, who was deferred or excluded, who died, who emigrated, and who answers surveys — the survey cannot split those. "Era" is a date range of service, not a deployment; the in-theater cohorts live in builds #15 and #16. The ASEC samples households, so the institutionalised and active-duty populations are absent.
