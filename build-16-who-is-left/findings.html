Education

Ten to fifteen years after separation, 44.7% of GWOT in-theater veterans hold a bachelor's degree or more against 38.7% of civilians the same age in the same years (+6.0 points, 95% CI +3.1 to +9.0); Vietnam in-theater veterans at the same distance were at 32.0% against 23.4% (+8.6 points, CI +4.9 to +12.3). By 16–22 years out the Vietnam advantage is gone (21.0% vs 22.0%) while GWOT holds (43.4% vs 41.7%, CI -3.6 to +6.9). The sharper difference is at the bottom: 1.1% of GWOT in-theater veterans lack a high-school diploma against 9.1% of their civilian peers (Cohen's h -0.40, a large effect); for Vietnam it was 7.9% vs 24.3% (h -0.46).

Race, ethnicity, sex

Both in-theater cohorts are whiter than civilians their age — Vietnam 80.0% white non-Hispanic vs 75.2% (+4.9 pts), GWOT 66.7% vs 57.9% (+8.8 pts) — and both under-represent Hispanics (GWOT 13.9% vs 19.9%, representation index 0.70; Vietnam 4.5% vs 9.2%, index 0.49). Black veterans sit near civilian parity in both wars. Women: 11.0% of the GWOT in-theater cohort against 1.6% of Vietnam's — the single largest change between the two armies, and still a fifth of the civilian share (52.6%).

Survival: what was tried, and why it cannot be read

The CPS weighted headcount of Vietnam in-theater veterans rises from 3.67 M in 1985 to 4.17 M in 1999. A closed cohort cannot grow; what grew is self-reported Vietnam service, on top of sampling and weighting noise and a 1997 change in who was asked. Deaths read as the gap between two surveys are therefore negative for most of the 1990s, which is not a finding about mortality but about the instrument. Where the series behaves (2007 on), the observed-to-expected ratios are 3.01 [1.87–4.15] for 2007→2011, 1.05 [0.18–1.92] for 2010→2015, 0.73 [-0.31–1.76] for 2011→2015, 1.21 [0.37–2.04] for 2015→2019: intervals that include 1.0 in most windows and each other in all of them. The GWOT closed cohort (in-theater, separated by 2009) is about 200 people a survey; its ratios run from negative to sixteen. The VA's VetPop2023 model retires Vietnam-era veterans at 4.3% a year (2023→24), a number built from mortality it assumes, not observes. Conclusion: a household survey cannot measure cohort mortality at this sample size; the right source is a death-record linkage (the VA's mortality data repository), which is not public microdata.

What this cannot say

Who enlists is upstream of everything here: the draft and the volunteer force selected different people on education before service began, and these are snapshots of survivors, not the people who joined. "In-theater" is the survey's flag, not a campaign medal. The survival leg is a negative result by design of the source, not of the cohorts: a household survey re-samples people every year and its respondents' answers about 1960s service drifted upward for a decade.
